**The call.** Buy Star Bulk at $28.98, 0.88x my asset NAV of $32.99, starting at 3% of NAV. The market prices it as a freight instrument. I think it is the best-run balance sheet in a commodity industry, run by a management team that buys ships and its own stock cheaply and sells both dearly. On my scenario weights the 12-month total return is about 20%, roughly 8 points of it cash. The honest bear case is -15% with no dividend. Add up to 5% below 0.80x NAV ($26.40) if the marks hold; trim at NAV ($33); exit on the triggers in section 6.

## 1. Franchise: why the lowest-cost, lightest balance sheet is the moat

Dry bulk has no pricing power. Every owner earns the same rate for the same ship-day. What separates owners is the cost of that day, the balance sheet they carry into a trough and the discipline to trade assets against the cycle. On all three, Star Bulk leads its closest US-listed peer.

| | Star Bulk | Genco (peer) |
|---|---|---|
| Fleet; deadweight | 135 ships; 13.5m t | 44 ships; 5.1m t |
| Average age (deadweight-weighted) | 13.2 years | 13.2 years |
| Vessel opex per ship-day (reported) | $5,265 (Q2 26) | $6,781 (H1 26) |
| Net debt / fleet value (my marks) | 10% | 21% |
| Scrubber-fitted (deadweight) | 97% | 15% |
| Average daily traded value, 3 months | $49m | $14m |

*Table 1. Fleets are as of each dated register; opex definitions differ slightly between companies.*

Same-age fleets, but Star Bulk runs a ship-day for about $1,500 less: procurement and overhead spread over three times the fleet. It enters any trough with low leverage and 29 unencumbered ships. The direction of travel is improving. Older ships are sold and replaced with newbuilds (three Kamsarmaxes delivered in Q2, five more due in H2), and 88% of the fleet now has energy-saving devices.

## 2. Stewardship: capital recycled against the cycle

The policy is explicit. Star Bulk pays out all operating cash flow after capex and debt service ($0.90 for Q2, 12.4% annualised) and has returned over $2.15bn since 2021. It bought back $46.3m of stock in H1 2026, $37.9m of it in Q1 when the shares traded between $19 and $27. In September it sold 4.4m new shares in Athens at EUR 24.50 (about $28.45). That price is 0.86x my NAV, which cost about 0.5% of NAV per share, but it sits above the whole range in which the company had been buying. The proceeds bought a second holder base and expected entry into the FTSE/ATHEX Large Cap index. Buying its own stock low and issuing it high is what I want from a cyclical steward. The risk is that it becomes a habit: the company renewed its at-the-market (ATM) share programmes in October, so ATM use below NAV is my first kill trigger.

## 3. Forensic test: what the market actually prices

On my valuation engine (finite ship lives, 10% discount rate, no unfinanced fleet replacement), Star Bulk needs freight 4.3% above my mid-cycle base vector to justify its price. Genco needs 13.8%. That looks like Genco is dear and Star Bulk cheap, but most of the gap is fleet mix. Genco is 71% Capesize/Newcastlemax against Star Bulk's 45%, and Cal-27 Capesize FFAs ($31.3k/day) sit 30% above my base. Reshape the rate vector to the forward curve and the market prices Star Bulk at 93% of that curve and Genco at 94%.

| Like-for-like test, 23 October 2026 | Star Bulk | Genco |
|---|---|---|
| Price / asset NAV | 0.88x | 0.94x |
| Freight uplift needed on my flat mid-cycle vector | +4.3% | +13.8% |
| Capesize + Newcastlemax share of deadweight | 45% | 71% |
| Share of the Cal-27 curve-shaped vector the price implies | 93% | 94% |

*Table 2. Same engine, discount rate and ship-life rules for both companies; each uses its own dated fleet, cost and debt inputs.*

Two conclusions follow:

* **Star Bulk is the cheapest way to own dry bulk on a like-for-like basis,** before giving any credit for its lower cost, lower leverage and liquidity. At a common share of the curve it would be worth $29.97, and its NAV discount (0.88x vs 0.94x) is the wider one.
* **The obvious hedge is a trap.** A Genco short would be mainly a short in Capesize rates, not a stock-specific hedge. If the freight beta needs trimming, I would sell Cal-27 Panamax FFAs against the midsize fleet, not short a peer.

## 4. Systems lens: buying a strong balance sheet near a possible peak

The bulker orderbook is 15.4% of the fleet. 2027 deliveries are 4.8% of the fleet against scrapping of about 0.3%, which points to a peak forming. Against that, Simandou (120 Mt/y nameplate) lengthens Atlantic-to-China iron ore hauls through 2028. I cannot call the cycle, and the thesis does not need me to. It rests on owning the operator whose balance sheet and capital discipline let it buy from weaker owners in a downturn. That is also why the downside case is NAV, not zero: in a trough, ships trade, and Star Bulk is a buyer.

## 6. What would make the thesis right, or wrong

**Right, and dated.** At Q3 results (November; date to be confirmed), buybacks resume below NAV, the ATM is unused and the full-payout dividend is kept. At the FTSE/ATHEX semi-annual review (November/December), Star Bulk is included. Through 2027, older-ship sales keep clearing near the marks, which confirms NAV.

**Wrong, and what I would do.** (1) Shares are issued through the ATM below 0.95x NAV: the stewardship case fails, so exit. (2) Sale-and-purchase prices or new appraisals imply marks more than 10% lower, enough to erase the NAV discount: cut to half. (3) Bulker ordering accelerates and the orderbook moves above about 20% of the fleet: the systems case weakens, so trim and reassess. (4) The price falls 20% without any of these: review against the marks before adding, and do not average down on freight alone.

## 7. Risks: a pre-mortem from October 2027, after the position lost 20%

* **Freight turned.** Deliveries landed, China steel output fell and rates fell towards my bear vector. Star Bulk stopped paying (bear-case cash is negative) and the stock fell to NAV or below. This is the main risk, and it is undiversifiable within the name.
* **The marks were wrong.** The appraisals date from June and August. A 5% error moves NAV by about 6.5% of the price, about half the base-case upside.
* **Stewardship slipped.** ATM issuance funded growth below NAV, or a large fleet acquisition near the peak used the balance sheet I am paying for.
* **The fuel spread narrowed.** With 97% of the fleet scrubber-fitted, a smaller gap between high- and low-sulphur fuel prices erodes Star Bulk's earnings premium.
* **The cash yield was overstated.** The 12.4% run-rate reflects Q2 rates. My base case pays 7.4%, and the third-party forward estimate (20.1%) assumes the current curve.

## 8. Sizing and portfolio fit

Start at 3% of NAV. Weekly volatility is about 34% annualised (three years) and beta to the S&P 500 about 1.05, so a 1-sigma year moves the fund by about 1%. Scale to 5% only on evidence (Q3 confirms the stewardship signals) or on price (below 0.80x NAV with marks intact). This is a cyclical position inside a long-biased book. If the portfolio already carries commodity or China exposure, I would remove some freight beta with Cal-27 Panamax FFAs rather than a peer short, for the reason in section 3.
